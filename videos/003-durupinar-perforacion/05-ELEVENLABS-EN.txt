=== PARTE 1 · HOOK · 0:00 ===

A few weeks ago, a team drilled into the ground at the place many people call Noah's Ark. Four or five meters down, the drill hit something so hard it broke.

Nobody knows yet what it is.

It's the first time in history anyone has been allowed to drill this deep into the Durupınar formation in eastern Turkey. More than sixty feet. Sixteen holes. Hundreds of samples now sitting in a lab.

The team says the results challenge the idea that this is just a natural rock formation. Geologists have said for thirty years that it is exactly that.

So today I'm going to show you what they actually found, what the radar can and can't prove, and the one detail almost every headline left out.


=== PARTE 2 · THE SHAPE FROM THE SKY · ~0:45 ===

The story starts in 1959. A captain in the Turkish army, İlhan Durupınar, was looking at aerial photographs of the mountains near the Iranian border.

About eighteen miles south of Mount Ararat, he saw something strange. A shape in the earth with a pointed end and a long body. It looked like the outline of a boat.

In 1960, the photo appeared in Life magazine, and an expedition went to see it in person. They used explosives to open the ground. They found earth and rock. Nothing else. They went home.

But the shape didn't go away. And neither did the question.


=== PARTE 3 · THE MEASUREMENTS · ~1:45 ===

The formation is about five hundred and fifteen feet long. One hundred and fifty-seven meters.

Genesis six, verse fifteen, says the ark was three hundred cubits long. If you use the common cubit, about eighteen inches, that's roughly four hundred and fifty feet.

But if you use the royal Egyptian cubit, which is a bit longer, three hundred cubits comes out to almost exactly five hundred and fifteen feet.

That's why people who believe this is the ark say it's a perfect match. And that's also why skeptics say it isn't: the match depends on which cubit you choose.

In 1987, Turkey protected the site as a cultural and natural heritage area. It built a visitor center. But it never declared that this was Noah's Ark. Officially, the question is still open.


=== PARTE 4 · THE GEOLOGISTS · ~2:50 ===

In the late seventies and eighties, an American amateur archaeologist named Ron Wyatt made Durupınar famous. He said he had found metal rivets, petrified deck timbers, and the giant stone anchors of the ark in a nearby village. He made the site famous around the world.

One of the men who worked next to him was a former merchant marine officer named David Fasold. He became one of the site's biggest believers. He even wrote a book about it.

Then, in 1996, he published a paper in the Journal of Geoscience Education, together with a geologist named Lorence Collins. And this is the detail almost nobody mentions: the believer changed his mind.

Their conclusion: it's a natural rock structure. A fold in the layers of the earth, called a syncline, shaped by erosion until it looked like a hull. The so-called iron brackets were natural minerals. The "fossilized wood" was rock.

For most scientists, that settled it. For thirty years, Durupınar was a tourist stop with a nice view of Ararat.

Until someone asked for permission to look underneath.


=== PARTE 5 · THE RADAR · ~4:00 ===

In 2026, the Turkish government approved the first invasive study of the site. The project is led by archaeologist Cenker Atila, of Sivas Cumhuriyet University, with about twenty researchers from five countries.

First, they scanned it with ground-penetrating radar. About six meters down, the radar showed shapes with straight edges. Some of them meet at right angles. The team calls them corridors and chambers.

Then the soil. The team says samples from inside the formation have about forty percent more carbon than samples from outside. Earlier tests, in 2024, found three times more organic matter inside.

Atila said the data, quote, "increase the possibility that this could be Noah's Ark with each passing day."

But here's what that radar can't do. It doesn't see wood. It doesn't see walls. It measures echoes, where one material changes into another. Rock, wet clay, an empty space, or a buried beam can all give an echo. Radar tells you something is there. It can't tell you what.

And more carbon doesn't give you a date. It's not carbon dating. A hollow in the ground that collects water and plants could have more organic matter on its own. That's why they needed to drill.


=== PARTE 6 · THE DRILL · ~5:30 ===

In September, the drilling began. A dry percussion drill, the kind used to go through soil and limestone.

On the second day, in one of the holes inside the formation, about four to five meters down, the drill hit a layer and stopped. The bit broke.

Andrew Jones, vice president of the project, said whatever they hit was "harder than limestone." The team says it could be petrified wood. Or something heavily mineralized. Or simply a hard block of rock. That's what the lab has to answer.

They kept going. Twelve holes inside the outline, four outside as a comparison. Some reached more than sixty feet. In one hole they found a cavity filled with mud and water.

And according to the team, inside the formation, they never hit bedrock. Jones said, quote, "We were hitting mainly soil."

Now, think about that for a second. The 1996 paper said this was a rock structure. The team says that, so far, they're finding mostly soil. That's the argument they're making.


=== PARTE 7 · WHAT THE HEADLINES LEFT OUT · ~7:00 ===

But there are three things you should know before you believe any headline.

One. Almost everything you've read comes from the team itself, through their own press releases. None of it has been peer reviewed yet.

Two. The man leading the international side doesn't hide what he thinks. Andrew Jones said, quote, "I believe this is Noah's Ark. That's why I'm putting the Durupınar site through the most rigorous testing possible." That's honest. But it means the team isn't neutral.

Three. There are no results yet. The samples are being tested this winter. They're looking for signs of human activity or decayed wood. The final report is expected next spring.

And the radar team only had seven days on site because of the rain. They're coming back in 2027 with a small camera drone called Gopher, named after the gopher wood in Genesis, to send down the holes.


=== PARTE 8 · CLOSE · ~8:30 ===

So, is it Noah's Ark? Today, nobody can tell you that honestly. Not the team, and not the people who laugh at them.

What we know is this: there's a boat-shaped formation on a mountain in Turkey. Something under it broke a drill. And sixty-seven years after that photo, the deepest samples ever taken from inside it are on their way to a lab.

When those lab results come out, I'll be here to tell you what they say. Not what the headlines say. What the lab says.
